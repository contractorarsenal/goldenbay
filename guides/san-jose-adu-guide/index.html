San Jose ADU Guide: Types, Permits and Fees

By Gold Bay Builders · Last reviewed 2026-10-15

Short answer. An accessory dwelling unit (ADU) is a self-contained home on a residential lot. State law requires California cities to allow ADUs on most residential lots, and the City of San José administers permits and fees locally. What you can build depends on your lot, so confirm specifics with the City before you commit.

The three common ADU types

TypeWhat it isTypical considerations
Detached ADUA new, separate building, usually in the backyard.Most privacy and design freedom; needs new foundation and utility runs.
Attached ADUA new unit built onto the main house.Shares a wall; connects to existing structure and sometimes existing utilities.
Garage conversionAn existing garage converted to living space.Uses the existing shell; floor, insulation, egress and utilities still need work. Parking implications should be checked.

A junior ADU (JADU) is a smaller unit created within the walls of the existing home. State law caps its size.

State law and the City’s role

California’s ADU statutes (Government Code, beginning at § 66310) limit how much cities can restrict ADUs and set deadlines for reviewing complete applications. Commonly cited protections include a minimum allowable size of 800 square feet and four-foot side and rear setbacks for qualifying units. The California Department of Housing and Community Development (HCD) publishes an ADU handbook and reviews local ordinances; the City of San José publishes its own ADU pages.

Treat any summary, including this one, as a starting point. Statutes change frequently, and your lot’s zoning and conditions can alter what applies.

Impact fees

The City of San José states that park and school impact fees do not apply to ADUs under 750 square feet. For ADUs of 750 square feet or more, applicants should contact the relevant school district and the City. A 2025 HCD review letter on San José’s ordinance describes state law as prohibiting impact fees on ADUs of 750 square feet or less and requiring proportionate fees for larger units. Legislation affecting fees on larger ADUs has been proposed, so verify current rules before budgeting.

Steps in an ADU project

  1. Feasibility. Confirm your lot size, existing structures, utilities and the ADU type that fits.
  2. Design and engineering. Produce permit-ready plans and any required structural, energy and utility documentation.
  3. Permit submittal. Submit to the City of San José Permit Center. Complete applications are subject to state review deadlines; incomplete ones restart the clock.
  4. Construction. Foundation, framing, mechanical/electrical/plumbing, insulation, finishes.
  5. Inspections and final. Required inspections and final approval before occupancy.

Checklist before you request bids

  • Your lot size and a survey or plot plan if available
  • Which ADU type you want and who it is for
  • Sewer, water, gas and electrical service locations and capacity
  • Whether the unit will be 750 square feet or smaller
  • Your budget range, including permits, fees and utility work

The 750 square foot line

The City describes the park and school fee exemption as applying to ADUs less than 750 square feet, while the state’s HCD describes the rule as 750 square feet or less. Because the wording differs, a unit designed comfortably under 750 square feet avoids the question. Confirm the measurement method (interior livable area) and current fee schedule with the City before finalizing a design.

Utility connections: the cost homeowners underestimate

The structure is only part of an ADU budget. Utility work depends on your lot and can change the total considerably:

  • Sewer. Distance to the sewer lateral or main, depth and any existing trees or structures in the path.
  • Water. Whether the existing service line and meter size can support a second home, and fire-sprinkler requirements that may apply.
  • Electrical. Whether the existing panel and service can carry the added load or need an upgrade.
  • Gas, drainage and grading. Trenching length, soil conditions and surface drainage around the new unit.

This is why we review the site before giving an estimate. We do not quote a utility figure without seeing the lot.

SB 9: duplexes and lot splits are a different path

SB 9 (2021) lets many single-family lots in California add a second primary unit, or split into two lots, under ministerial approval and with conditions, including owner-occupancy affidavits for lot splits. It is separate from ADU law, has its own eligibility rules (zoning, historic and hazard overlays, tenant history) and is administered locally. HCD’s fact sheet summarizes the rules; the City determines whether your lot qualifies.

How long does review take?

State law sets a 60-day review deadline for complete ADU applications. Real-world schedules depend on how complete the submittal is, correction cycles, and the type of project. Complex ADUs and additions can take longer, and we do not promise a timeline. Ask the City for current typical review times.

What we do not promise

We do not guarantee permit approval dates, rental income or property-value increases. These depend on the City, the market and your property.
