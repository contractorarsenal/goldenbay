A home addition adds living space by building outward or upward, which means new foundation or framing, a roof tie-in, and connections to existing plumbing, electrical and HVAC.

Overview

What a home addition involves

Additions come in two broad forms: expanding outward on the lot (a bump-out, a new wing) or upward (a second story). Each changes the foundation, framing and roof, and each must tie into existing plumbing, electrical and heating and cooling.

The hardest part is the connection point, where new construction meets the old. That junction determines whether the result feels original to the house or bolted on.

Common projects

Typical work in this category.

  • Primary suite addition

    A new bedroom and bath, often on the ground floor.

  • Second-story addition

    Adds floor area without using more of the lot, but requires evaluating the existing foundation and walls.

  • Family room or kitchen expansion

    Extends the main living area outward.

  • Multigenerational space

    Adds bedrooms and bathrooms for relatives, sometimes with a separate entrance.

Homeowner concerns

Questions that decide an addition

  • Will the lot allow it?

    Setbacks, height limits and coverage rules vary by zone. This is checked before design money is spent.

  • Can the existing structure carry it?

    Second stories in particular require an engineer to evaluate foundations and framing below.

  • Will it match?

    Siding, roofing, windows and rooflines all need to line up for the addition to read as part of the original.

What shapes scope and cost

  • Footprint versus second story: building up avoids new foundation area but may require strengthening what is below.
  • Mechanical capacity: your existing electrical panel and HVAC may need upgrading to serve the new space.
  • Site access for equipment and materials, which varies a great deal on narrow San Jose lots.
  • Permit and plan-review requirements, which are set by your city and take time.

What helps us give an accurate estimate

  • A sketch or description of the space you want
  • Your lot survey or plot plan if you have one
  • Photos of the area where the addition would attach
  • Whether you have an architect or designer
  • Your preferred start window

Questions

Home Additions: frequently asked

Is a second-story addition or a ground-level addition better?

It depends on your lot, budget and existing structure. Ground-level additions use yard space; second stories preserve it but need the existing structure evaluated.

Do additions require an architect?

Additions need permit-ready plans, and often structural engineering. Those can come from an architect, a designer or a plan drafter. We can discuss options.

How long does an addition take?

Design and permitting often take longer than construction, and the city controls much of that timing. We provide a schedule once scope and plans are defined.

Will an addition affect my property taxes?

It can, since new construction may be reassessed. Ask the Santa Clara County Assessor’s Office for specifics on your property.
